François Truffaut famously said that there is no such thing as an anti-war film, arguing that every movie about war ends up being pro-war because to show something on screen is, in some way, to ennoble or glamorize it.

When you look at *Warfare* (2025), directed by Ray Mendoza and Alex Garland, it initially seems to fit squarely into Truffaut’s paradox. It portrays the absolute horrors of combat in Ramadi, Iraq, but at no point does it take an overt, didactic political stance. You never hear two soldiers sitting in the dust questioning the geopolitical machinery behind the invasion. It simply depicts what happened, refusing to wrap itself in a neat anti-war moralizing bow.

If a film is purely documenting and journalizing real-world memories, then it ought to be completely down the middle. And compared to 90% of Hollywood war movies, *Warfare* practically strips away conventional cinematic glamorization. For instance, Will Poulter’s character—suffering from a severe concussion and completely overwhelmed—asks a captain from another squad to take over command. In terms of traditional Hollywood movie heroism, where leaders are expected to push forward even with their limbs hanging off, this looks like cowardice. But *Warfare* isn't interested in making a Hollywood action hero; it is sticking strictly to the truth of what these men experienced. It isn't a reflection on character cowardice, but rather an unvarnished window into human limits under extreme duress.

Yet, as close as the film gets to raw, it can’t entirely escape Truffaut’s trap. There are distinct elements where the film accidentally edges into glamorization, particularly in its second half, for two major reasons:

1. **The terrifying efficiency of elite military capability:** Because the film is dedicated to being brutally true to life, it showcases just how impossibly well-trained the U.S. military is. Watching these squads communicate entirely in precise tactical jargon, grid coordinates, and seamless tactical execution is mesmerizing. It is fascinating to watch a high-level killing machine operate with such next-level precision. Even though the military is a horrific engine of death, it is hard to watch professional competence at that scale without thinking, *“Okay, these guys are absolute badasses.”*
2. **The rescue and the value of life:** These men are trapped in an impossible nightmare, desperately needing backup. When that second squad finally pushes in to save them, it is inherently heroic. Furthermore, notice how the film handles casualties: unlike standard action cinema where nameless, faceless enemies are mowed down by the hundreds without a second thought, *Warfare* keeps enemy deaths sparse and off-camera for much of the first half, deliberately subverting the casual dehumanization common in war tropes. But when the second squad arrives, we see a captain take up a street position and open fire, and it looks undeniably badass. Because the narrative is built directly from the memory of the first squad, the arrival of their saviors *was* heroic in real life. Risking your life to pull your brothers out of a slaughterhouse is genuine heroism.

This brings us back to the core question: **Is it glamorizing something just to tell the truth?**

The truth is that these men *were* genuinely heroic in real life, not because of a scriptwriter's imagination, but because of how they looked out for each other under fire. Showing real courage sitting right next to raw fear isn't artificial glamorization—it's historical honesty.

As for Truffaut’s famous quote, I don't know if I would label *Warfare* a neat, text-book anti-war film, but I certainly wouldn't call it pro-war either. Any sane person watching its suffocating, hellish intensity would never want to live through it. Yet it also celebrates the profound bond and survival of these men. Ultimately, *Warfare* transcends simple labels by being a radically honest film. It reminds us that none of the soldiers on that street died because of choices they made; those catastrophic decisions were made miles away by leadership sitting far above them. By stripping away political preachy-ness, the film leaves us with something much more powerful: a profound sympathy for human beings caught in a machine they couldn't control, and an undeniable testament to the courage they showed for one another.
