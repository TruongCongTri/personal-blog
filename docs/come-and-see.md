François Truffaut famously said that there is no such thing as an anti-war film, arguing that by the sheer act of depicting war on screen, a filmmaker ends up glorifying it.

If there is one movie in cinema history that people point to in order to prove Truffaut wrong, it is Elem Klimov’s Soviet masterpiece Come and See (1985). And for almost its entire runtime, it genuinely seems to escape Truffaut’s trap. Think about how most war movies—even self-proclaimed anti-war movies—rely on the adrenaline rush of combat. Just like in a James Bond flick where the hero gets dramatically injured before mowing down three hundred faceless bad guys, standard war cinema relies on the thrill of fighting back and taking down the enemy. Come and See strips that away completely. We follow a fourteen-year-old Belarusian boy named Flyora who eagerly joins the Soviet partisans with a rifle in his hands, yet up until the very last scene of the movie, he never shoots and kills a single German soldier on screen. There are no "badass" firefights, no heroic tactical maneuvers, and zero moments where you lean forward in your chair thinking, "Oh man, that's cool."

Instead of cinematic heroism, Flyora is defined by sheer, overwhelming terror and cowardice. When the German bombers attack, he doesn't stand his ground—he panics and runs away into the woods, desperately hiding in the dirt. Even when he is ordered to go out and find food for starving villagers, he is paralyzed by fear, running away from danger rather than confronting it. Deafened by artillery, trudging helplessly through swamps, and watching in frozen horror as an SS unit herds an entire village into a wooden church and burns them alive, Flyora is the exact opposite of a Hollywood soldier.

And so, if a film refuses to show its protagonist killing the enemy in combat—and instead shows him running away in terror—surely it has solved Truffaut's problem, right? There shouldn't be any glamorization in that.

Yet at the very end of the film, we get a deeply jarring, confusing scene: as the partisans move on, Flyora spots a framed portrait of Adolf Hitler lying in a muddy puddle. For the first time in the movie, Flyora raises his rifle and fires—shooting the puddle over and over again. As he fires, archival footage flashes on screen, rewinding history backwards through World War II, through Hitler’s rise to power, through World War I, all the way back to a photograph of Hitler as an infant sitting in his mother’s lap. At that moment, Flyora stops shooting, lowers his rifle, and breaks down weeping.

How do we read that scene? Is it a sudden slip into glamorization—finally giving the audience that cinematic release of pulling a trigger and symbolically destroying evil—or is it simply an honest reflection of what a traumatized Soviet soldier would desperately want to do after enduring an unimaginable amount of horror? I think it is the latter. After watching women and children burned alive and seeing his youth destroyed in a matter of days, firing blindly at an image of the man responsible isn't "action-hero badassery"; it is the impotent, psychological breakdown of a child drowning in grief. And by having Flyora stop when he sees baby Hitler, the film insists that despite everything he has suffered, this Soviet boy refuses to kill an innocent life.

**And that is the exact point where the film’s honesty falters—and where we have to criticize *Come and See* for what it chooses to leave out.**

Throughout the film, Klimov uses the brutalization of women by German soldiers as the ultimate symbol of war's depravity. We see Glasha traumatized, and we see a young Belarusian woman dragged onto the back of a German flatbed truck, stripped, and gang-raped before being left in a daze with harmonica in her mouth. It is horrific, and it happened in real life.

But the true horror of war—and the ultimate failure of humanity in wartime—is how the suffering inflicted on women flips from one side to the other almost overnight. At the end of *Come and See*, Flyora marches back into the forest with hundreds of armed Soviet partisans, heading west toward Berlin. The film stops right there, letting the audience believe that because Flyora couldn't bring himself to shoot a picture of a baby, the Soviet march into Germany was purely a righteous liberation by broken, moral men.

History tells a brutal, terrifyingly different story: **the Rape of Berlin.** When those traumatized, vengeful Soviet soldiers crossed onto German soil in 1945, they inflicted the exact same monstrous nightmare upon millions of German women—including an estimated 100,000 women in Berlin alone.

Modern viewers often carry a misconception about wartime sexual violence, assuming that mass rape only happened to poor, lower-class women who were trapped in the rubble without protection. In the fall of Berlin, class meant nothing. Overnight, the entire social hierarchy collapsed. Aristocratic women, doctors, wives of high-ranking officials, and wealthy upper-class German women were hunted down in basements and luxury villas alike. High-society women were forced to barter their bodies to Soviet officers for a loaf of bread or protection, knowing full well that if they refused, they would be dragged out, gang-raped by dozens of soldiers in succession, and shot dead on the spot. From eight-year-old girls to eighty-year-old grandmothers, across every single social class, women's bodies became the final battlefield of the Eastern Front.

If the defense of a war film is *"it’s not glamorizing to show something exactly how it existed,"* then selective truth becomes its own form of glamorization. By ending the story with Flyora sparing the image of baby Hitler, *Come and See* morally glamorizes the Soviet soldier—framing him as an eternal victim whose suffering kept his soul pure.

If a movie truly wants to be an uncompromising *anti-war* film—and if it specifically uses the sexual violation of women to show the depths of war's evil—then it has a responsibility to show that horror on **both sides**. War does not divide neatly into monsters on one side and saints on the other; the machinery of war takes traumatized boys like Flyora and turns them into the very men who terrorized the women of Berlin a year later.

As Truffaut goes, there is no such thing as an anti-war film. I don't know if I could describe *Come and See* as a complete anti-war film when it only indicts the atrocities of the invader while shielding the atrocities of the victor. It is an unforgettable masterpiece about the *Nazi* horror in Belarus, and every person who endured that hell deserves our deepest sympathy. But by refusing to show how the cycle of vengeance consumed women on both sides of the front line, even the bleakest war movie ever made proves Truffaut right: the moment a camera chooses where to cut to black, it is no longer just telling the whole truth—it is protecting a side.
